Key discoveries

Where research changed the direction of the design.

01 · Control panel

People want every cycle in view.

“I just want to see all my cycles, without scrolling to find them.”

Assumption
A hidden-until-lit panel would feel more premium.
Evidence
With all cycles visible, people finished tasks faster and more often (A/B test, n = 12).
Impact
The team chose the all-cycles-visible panel.

02 · App interaction

Monthly savings motivate more than per-cycle data.

“One wash doesn’t matter. I want to know what I can save, and what I saved, on electricity and water this month.”

Assumption
Per-cycle energy and water data would drive sustainable choices.
Evidence
Per-cycle data helped people choose; monthly savings felt more meaningful (concept testing).
Impact
The app added monthly savings and off-peak scheduling.

03 · Premium design

Premium is felt as well as seen.

“It should look high-end, and still be easy to use.”

Assumption
Premium means minimal: no knob, no text.
Evidence
People linked premium to color and hidden drawers, and a knob felt easier to use (interviews and EU/NA benchmarks).
Impact
Shaped the CMF strategy; desirability rose in later testing.

Also found

Premium ≠ preferredWhat looks premium isn’t always what people want to live with.
Longevity is the silent decision-maker“Will this age well?” decided what people would actually buy.

04 · Controls

Visible options matter more than a big screen.

Assumption
Every tier should move to a full touchscreen.
Evidence
Cap touch led on appeal and usability (NA survey, ~250).
Impact
Touch panels in upper tiers, a knob in lower tiers.
